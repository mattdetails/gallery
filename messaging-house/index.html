Messaging House Frameworks

Graphyte · Research notes · 2026

How to build a messaging house

What brand experts use to decide who you talk to, what you say, and how you say it. And what that means for a small business's messaging house.

The short version

A messaging house is a one-page tool. The roof is one main sentence. The pillars are the three main things you want people to know. The base is the proof behind each pillar.

The house mostly covers what you say. It barely covers who you're talking to, and it skips how you sound. Other tools fill those gaps.

Every tool we looked at agrees on three things:

  1. Describe your reader by the moment they're in. Age and job title matter less.
  2. Keep to three main points. Drop any claim you can't prove.
  3. Use plain words. Use your customer's own words when you can.

The middle of the process matters most. Good copy comes from a short page packed with real examples: customer quotes, founder quotes, words to use and avoid, and proof. People and AI both write better from examples than from adjectives like "friendly" or "bold".

1. What a messaging house is

PR teams started using the messaging house around the 1990s. They needed everyone in a company to say the same thing, in every place.

It has three parts:

  • Roof. One sentence that sums up your value. It must be true, set you apart, and work for any reader.
  • Pillars. Three main messages, four at most. Each is a different reason to care. They shouldn't overlap.
  • Base. Proof for each pillar: numbers, examples and quotes from customers.

The most useful rule is about proof:

If a pillar has no proof, go and get the proof or cut the pillar.

Why three pillars? People remember things in threes. One guide says three is ideal, four is OK, two is too few and five is too many.

How long? One sentence for the roof. One short paragraph per pillar. A few bullet points of proof.

What it leaves out

The basic house has no part for tone or personality. Some people bolt on a tone section. Some add a "basement" for values and mission. The core house is only about what you say.

Common mistakes

  • Too many pillars
  • Claims with no proof
  • A roof full of jargon. The fix is to read it to someone outside the business.
  • Different versions floating around

Big companies spend weeks and a full-day workshop on one. A small business owner has a weekend. That's why the house needs a simple guide.

2. Other brand tools feed the house

Three words get mixed up a lot:

  • Positioning is the choice: what you are, who it's for, and why it's better.
  • Messaging is the words that get that choice across.
  • Value proposition is the promise you make to customers.

The house turns positioning into words people can use.

The main tools

There are lots of positioning tools. They look different, but they ask for the same few things.

  • Geoffrey Moore (1991). A fill-in-the-blanks sentence: "For [customer] who [need]… Unlike [other option]…"
  • April Dunford. Start with what the customer would use instead, including doing nothing. Then what's special about you, the value, who it's for, and what kind of thing it is.
  • Unilever's Brand Key. Adds an "insight" written the way customers talk, reasons to believe you, a personality, and a 3 to 6 word "essence".
  • StoryBrand. The customer is the hero with a problem. The business is the guide who hands them a plan.
  • Brand wheel and brand pyramid. Climb from features, to benefits, to values, to personality.
  • Simon Sinek's Golden Circle. Say why first, then how, then what. It's about order, not content.

3. Who: describe the moment, not the person

Age and job title don't tell you why someone buys. The moment they're in does.

Knowing someone's age doesn't explain why they bought a Snickers bar. Having 30 seconds to grab something that stops hunger for 30 minutes does.

That example is Alan Klement's. Here are the tools built on the same idea.

  • Job stories. "When [moment], I want to [goal], so I can [result]."
  • Value Proposition Canvas. The customer's tasks, what hurts, and what they hope to gain. Its warning: a fit is only a guess until customers say yes.
  • The four forces. Two things push people to change: the pain of today and the pull of a better life. Two things hold them back: worry about the new thing, and habit. Good copy turns up the push and pull, and turns down the worry and habit. The pull is the better life the customer pictures. It isn't your feature list.

Are personas still useful?

Yes, a little. Two groups can do the same job and care about different things. A builder and a homeowner both drill holes. One cares that it lasts. The other cares about price. Most personas fail because nobody uses them after they're made.

The best mix is to picture one reader, then fill in their moment:

  • What set them off
  • What hurts
  • What they want
  • What worries them
  • What they do today
  • How much they already know
  • Their exact words

How much they know decides the headline

Eugene Schwartz sorted readers into five levels. Each one needs a different opening.

The reader…Lead with…
Knows you and is ready to buyThe product and the price
Knows your productClaims and proof
Knows solutions existThe result and how you get it
Only knows the problemName the problem
Doesn't know they have a problemA story

Rule of One. One idea, one reader, one promise, one call to action. If you talk to many readers at once, you can't be specific for any of them. April Dunford says the same: start with a reader as narrow as you can.

Use your customers' own words

Joanna Wiebe's "review mining" is the best way to collect them.

  1. Find six or more products or books that solve the same problem.
  2. Read the 2, 3 and 4 star reviews. That's where the honest detail is.
  3. Work in 20-minute bursts. Grab about 20 phrases each time.
  4. Tag each phrase as a problem, a pain or a solution.
  5. Look for the same thing said the same way, again and again.

It works. Reviews of six addiction books gave this headline:

"If you think you need rehab, you do."
400%+more clicks on the button, plus over 20% more forms filled in.

Wynter puts the goal simply: join the conversation already going on in your customer's head.

4. How: voice, tone and word lists

Give each voice trait a limit

Each trait says what to be, and how far is too far.

  • Mailchimp: "weird but not inappropriate". Also: "It's always more important to be clear than entertaining."
  • Shopify: "Be real, but not too tough or overly familiar."
  • GOV.UK: "brisk, but not terse" and "serious but not pompous".

The limit tells a writer when they've gone too far.

NN/g sets tone on four sliders:

  • Formal to casual
  • Serious to funny
  • Respectful to cheeky
  • Matter-of-fact to excited

Voice stays the same, tone changes

Mailchimp says it best: "You have the same voice all the time, but your tone changes."

  • Monzo turns kindness and jokes up or down by channel. Account messages get full kindness and no jokes. Social posts get full jokes.
  • Atlassian gives worried or angry readers calm, clear help. Happy readers get a little fun.

Trust matters most

NN/g tested tone on insurance, bank, hospital and security websites.

52%of whether people liked a site came down to trust. Friendliness added only 8%.

A jokey insurance site felt friendlier but less trustworthy. Casual, chatty and a bit upbeat did best.

Word lists need a swap and a reason

GOV.UK keeps a list of about 30 words to avoid, each with a plain swap:

Don't saySay
leverageuse
keyimportant (unless you mean a real key)
transformsay what actually changed
80%of people preferred plain English. Experts liked it even more.

Shopify bans "every", "all" and "most" because they overpromise.

For a small business, keep the list short. Group the words by reason: jargon, hype, hedging and cold legal talk. Start with words the founder already avoids.

Capture the founder's voice from samples

Ghostwriters don't ask for facts. They ask for stories, like "Take me to the day you decided to start your company." They read casual emails and texts. They listen for sentence length, repeated words, pet phrases and humour.

Velocity Partners adds a warning: you can't have a voice until you have an opinion.

AI tools found the same thing:

  • Writer builds a voice from 300 to 1,000 words of samples. It says voices built from examples beat voices written as descriptions.
  • Jasper builds a voice from three to five samples.
  • "Friendly but professional" describes half the internet. Teams without a clear guide spend 15 to 30 minutes fixing the tone of every AI draft.

5. From the house to real words

The house doesn't write copy. It's a brief. Writers turn it into lines for each place they'll appear.

Write in set lengths

PieceLength
TaglineA few words
Headline10 to 15 words
One-linerProblem, then solution, then result
Elevator pitch1 to 2 sentences
Description100 to 200 words, with proof

Teams reuse the elevator pitch, short summaries (50, 120 and 250 words) and company blurbs most.

The few-seconds test. A visitor should know three things fast: what you offer, how it makes life better, and what to do next.

Clear beats clever. Emma Stratton says a headline must clearly say what you are.

Judge lines with cheap checks first

Copyhackers says to write at least 50 headlines before you pick. Then score each one:

  • Does it match what the visitor expects?
  • Does it grab attention without being sleazy?
  • Is it clear?
  • Does it get to the point?
  • Does it show a benefit?

Harry Dry's three questions are even faster:

  1. Can you picture it?
  2. Can you prove it wrong?
  3. Could nobody else say it?

These turn "32GB storage" into "1,000 songs in your pocket". They kill "quality you can trust".

Then one costly check: ask real people. With Wynter panels, the answers stop changing after 12 to 13 people. A/B tests need about 500 sales a month, and they tell you which line won but not why.

Don't let AI pick the winner

A study looked at 399 real headline tests from Upworthy. It asked AI to guess which headline really won.

49%right when the AI judged as itself.
35%right when the AI pretended to be ten different readers. Playing a role made it worse.

Another firm, Maslansky, found fake AI readers matched real people only 30% to 50% of the time, "not much better than a coin flip". Use fake readers for brainstorming only. Checking a draft against a word list is fine. That's simple matching.

Keep a library, not a PDF

  • Store each piece on its own: value lines, pain phrases, proof, and answers to common doubts.
  • Tag each piece with the reader, where it's used, and the buying stage.
  • Show when each piece was last checked, and by whom.
  • Add new proof when something good happens, like a big win.
  • Store copy by length and place, as well as by pillar.

Guidelines get ignored when they're stale PDFs, or when they say "be friendly" with no examples.

6. What a small business's house should hold

A simple process of inputs, refining and outputs fits the research well, with one change. The inputs are simple to collect, but they should be full of real words. The house is only as good as the customer and founder words that go in.

The middle is where the frameworks do their work. It makes a one-page house and a one-page voice sheet. What comes out is plain words in set lengths.

WHO

One reader, described by their moment and their words

Goes in

  • One main reader per house. More readers means more houses. Never blend them.
  • The moment that sets them off
  • What hurts about how they do it now
  • The result they want
  • Their biggest worry about switching
  • What they do today, or doing nothing
  • How much they already know
  • 20 to 40 exact phrases from reviews, emails, calls and enquiries, each tagged

Refining

  • Group the quotes until the same phrases keep showing up. These become headline ideas and the pillar order.
  • Write one job story.
  • Name the push and the pull in the reader's words.
  • Note how much they know. It decides what the headline leads with.
  • Keep the reader narrow. Widen later.

Comes out

  • A one-paragraph reader profile a stranger could write a headline from
  • The job story
  • How much they know
  • The tagged quote bank, shown next to every output so the client sees where each line came from
WHAT

A roof, three pillars, and only claims you can prove

Goes in

  • What you are, in plain words
  • What the reader would pick instead, including nothing
  • The one benefit you can prove
  • Two or three more benefits
  • Every bit of proof you have: numbers, named customers, reviews, guarantees, before-and-after stories. For a small business this list is short, and that's fine.

Refining

  • Write the roof: one sentence on what you are and why it matters, in the reader's words. Read it to someone outside the business.
  • Pick three pillars. Each one is a different reason to switch. Four at most.
  • Put proof under each pillar. No proof means cut it, or go and get proof.
  • Add a "we stand for" line only if the founder has a real opinion.
  • Check every line. Can you picture it? Can it be proved wrong? Could a competitor paste it on their site?

Comes out

  • The one-page house: roof, three pillars with a paragraph each, and proof under each
  • The set lengths: tagline, headline and subhead, one-liner, elevator pitch, and a 100 to 200 word description
  • Every piece tagged with its pillar and reader, and dated
HOW

One voice, a tone dial, and a word list with reasons

Goes in

  • Five to ten exact founder quotes from casual writing or a story chat. No polished marketing copy.
  • The founder's pet phrases and repeated words
  • Words the founder won't use
  • A point of view: something they believe that competitors wouldn't say

Refining

  • Pick three voice traits, each with a limit, like "direct but not blunt". Take them from the samples. Don't make them up.
  • Set the four tone sliders. Add a few "not like this" words.
  • Set the tone for each place the client writes: homepage, enquiry reply, bad-news email, social post. Jokes go in marketing, never in errors or apologies.
  • Build the word list as "say this, not that" pairs, grouped by reason.
  • Keep the whole voice sheet to one page.

Comes out

  • The voice sheet: traits with limits, tone for each place, and the word list
  • Three to five examples with notes, including one before-and-after rewrite
  • An automatic check that flags any line with a "don't say" word

How the three fit together

  1. Who comes first. The customer quotes feed both the roof and the word list.
  2. What comes next. The pillars decide what gets written.
  3. How comes last. Voice gets applied to real lines. It isn't invented in the abstract.

Every line should show where it came from: which quote, which pillar, which reader. That's what makes it feel like the client's words and not the tool's.

Keep the library dated, and tag it by length and place. Give the client two small habits. Add proof when something good happens. Add a quote when a customer says something that sticks.

AI can write lots of options. It shouldn't pick the winner. A five-second test on a stranger, or a small group of real readers, should.

7. What this means in practice

The frameworks are mostly the same checklist at different sizes. The messaging house is the smallest honest version of the "what" part.

The real value is in the raw material: customer phrases, founder quotes, proof and a short word list. The house and the voice sheet squeeze that material down, and the copy comes from it. A process that keeps the raw material visible all the way to the final line will beat one that hides it behind a polished page.

The middle of the process should be strict and simple:

  • One reader
  • Three pillars
  • No claims without proof
  • Voice traits with limits
  • A word list under a page

Most of the pieces in the middle are checks. They test lines more than they write them. Let AI draft lots of options, and let the house decide what gets through.
